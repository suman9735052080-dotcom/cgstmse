Credit Guarantee Research · Chapter 00

Did India's credit guarantees truly reach the unbanked
—or merely insure the banks that lent?

Fifteen years. 11.5 million guarantees. ₹9.35 lakh crore sanctioned. A pandemic rescue of ₹3.68 lakh crore under ECLGS. This dashboard confronts the central tension in India's CGS architecture: the trade-off between credit access, financial inclusion, and the moral hazard quietly accumulating inside public-sector lenders.

01 · Access

The democratisation of formal credit — or its façade?

CGTMSE was designed as a risk-transfer mechanism to unlock collateral-free lending for the "missing middle" of Indian industry. Over seventeen years, guarantees approved grew from 53,708 to 27.15 lakh per year. The amount guaranteed expanded 138-fold. By any measure, the plumbing of credit access was rebuilt.

02 · Inclusion

Who actually benefits?
Turnover, jobs — and the representation gap

Beyond loan counts, CGTMSE's impact disclosures tell a second story: 303 lakh jobs attributed to guaranteed units by FY25, ₹83.3 lakh crore in estimated turnover, ₹28,066 crore in exports. Yet women remain stuck at 22% of beneficiaries and the Northeast at 3% — flat for four consecutive years. Scale has not yet translated into representational inclusion.

Evidence · Positive

CGTMSE has become the de facto collateral-substitute for India's "missing middle"

The FY25 average ticket of ₹11.25 lakh sits squarely in the segment that banks historically refused to lend to without title deeds. The scheme now covers 10 distinct lender categories — from SBI to a cooperative bank in Tripura — giving guarantee-backed credit genuine last-mile reach. Employment and turnover scale-ups are consistent with this expansion, even discounting for self-reporting bias.

Evidence · Cautionary

Volume masks persistent exclusion gaps

Four sobering patterns: women remain capped at 21–22% for four straight years; NER is 3% — below the region's 8% population share; 84% of NABSanrakshan/CGSFPO guarantees route through one NABARD-subsidiary NBFC; and the MSME share of GBC (6.37% in FY25) has not reclaimed its FY14 peak of 8.40%. Guarantees are deep — but they are not yet wide.

03 · Risk

The NPA spike —
how bad did MSE credit quality really get?

Three independent measures of credit quality all tell the same story. MSE NPAs in scheduled commercial banks climbed from ₹15,638 cr (FY11) to a peak of ₹128,502 cr (FY21) — a 8.2× expansion. The ratio of MSE NPAs to MSME credit exploded from 4.79% to 22.47%, the highest on record. Then, just as dramatically, they have receded.
MSE NPAs — decomposed by bank group

Peak: ₹1.29 L cr · FY21
The peak in FY2020-21 coincides precisely with ECLGS launch and RBI moratorium forbearance. PSBs carried 79% of total MSE NPAs at peak despite holding 58% of MSME credit — consistent with H2 (moral hazard). As banks cleaned books via write-offs and SARFAESI, the aggregate fell to ₹95,238 cr (FY25) — still 6.1× the FY11 level.

The MSE-specific ratio crossed 20% in FY19 — before COVID — signaling that the deterioration began with demonetisation and GST, not the pandemic. The 22.5% peak masks what would have been higher without ECLGS 100% cover absorbing rollovers.

Annual ₹ crore · sustainability check
Self-financing
FY25 claims (₹3,101 cr) exceed guarantee fees (₹1,607 cr) — but service fees, interest on the FD corpus, and recoveries together dwarf claims. Total income ₹5,604 cr vs claims + opex ≈ ₹3,125 cr. The scheme remains decisively cash-positive.

The MSE NPA ratio was already past 20% two years before the pandemic. ECLGS didn't cause the stress — it stopped the bleed, at the cost of putting ₹3.68 lakh crore of taxpayer-backed cover onto the balance sheet.

— Central Finding 2 of 4
04 · Moral Hazard

PSBs, PVBs, and the H2 test —
does guarantee cover change lending behaviour?

The classical test: if guarantee cover induces moral hazard, the banks most reliant on guarantees should see higher MSE NPA ratios than their less-reliant peers. Public Sector Banks hold 58% of CGTMSE-guaranteed accounts. Their MSE NPA-to-Total-GNPA ratio has been consistently higher than Private Banks' in every single post-AQR year — and the gap widened post-COVID. The data supports H2.
PSB vs PVB — MSE NPAs as share of total GNPAs
The moral hazard proxy: when guaranteed, banks book MSE losses rather than other losses
H2: Supported
The PSB-PVB gap widened from 2.5pp (FY16) to 9.7pp (FY25). As PSBs cleaned their corporate books post-AQR, MSE NPAs rose as a share of what remained — indicating reluctance or inability to equally resolve guarantee-covered loans. This is the clearest econometric fingerprint of moral hazard in the dataset.
The moral hazard gauge
PSB MSE/Total NPA ratio minus PVB equivalent, percentage points
Gap bottomed at 1.3pp in FY18 (height of corporate NPA clean-up) and has trebled since. Not a story of fraud — a story of asymmetric resolution incentives.

A ratio approaching 1.0× means fees collected barely cover claims paid — before recoveries, interest, and subsidy. CGFSD at 2.53× (Stand-Up Dalit loans) is already in structural deficit. CGFMU at 0.95× is the canary in the coal mine.

05 · ECLGS & the COVID Break

Fire-hose, or fiscal firewall?
The ₹3.68 lakh crore intervention

Announced May 2020, ECLGS was the largest credit guarantee operation in Indian history: 100% sovereign cover, additional working capital, no collateral. By FY24 (scheme closed), ₹3.68 lakh crore stood sanctioned across 1.19 crore units. NCGTC's committed corpus expanded 18.8×. MSME credit jumped 14.96% YoY in FY21 — the single largest one-year expansion on record.

06 · Sustainability

Is the architecture self-financing —
or a contingent fiscal liability waiting to activate?

CGTMSE today runs a ₹32,730 crore balance sheet against a ₹16,500 crore corpus. Its operating expenses are ₹23 crore. Annual income of ₹5,604 crore covers claims 1.8×. By its own numbers, the scheme is durable. The risk is concentrated in off-balance sheet outstanding guarantees — and in whether a second COVID-scale shock would trigger simultaneous invocations across ECLGS, CGFMU, and CGTMSE.

07 · Synthesis

The balanced verdict:
access wins. hazard is real. sustainability holds —
for now.

We return to the central research question. The answer is emphatically not binary. The evidence across 15 fiscal years, 9 data sources, and 4 scheme families supports a nuanced diagnosis.

What the schemes achieved

  • 01
    Credit access expanded dramatically. CGTMSE approvals grew 50.6×, sanction 138×, with the biggest gains after FY17 Rebooting and FY22 post-ECLGS push.
  • 02
    Last-mile reach improved. MLI base expanded to 276 lenders across 10 categories — from cooperative banks to NBFCs to MFIs. Geographic penetration is meaningful, if uneven.
  • 03
    ECLGS prevented a crisis. The ₹3.68 lakh crore sanction pushed MSME credit growth to 39.8% in FY22 and stabilised a credit stock that had been flat since FY15. No reasonable counterfactual shows better.
  • 04
    Architecture is financially sound. CGTMSE balance sheet ₹32,730 cr, corpus ₹16,500 cr, operating expenses ₹23 cr. Income covers claims 1.8×. NCGTC PAT growing 55.9% CAGR.
  • 05
    Real economy footprint. Guaranteed units support 303 lakh jobs and ₹83.3 lakh crore in estimated turnover — even discounted heavily, the contribution to formal employment is material.

Where the schemes struggle

  • 01
    Moral hazard is empirically detectable. PSB MSE NPA/Total GNPA ratio is 9.7pp higher than PVBs in FY25 — vs 1.3pp in FY18. The divergence tracks CGTMSE reliance.
  • 02
    NPA spike was severe. MSE NPAs hit 22.47% of MSME credit in FY21 — the highest recorded. ECLGS absorbed the cash-flow shock but the ratio revealed existing balance-sheet fragility.
  • 03
    Inclusion remains shallow. Women are stuck at 22%, NER at 3%, both flat since FY22. MSME share of GBC (6.37%) still trails the FY14 peak of 8.40%.
  • 04
    Sister-scheme actuarial strain. CGFSD claims-to-fees already at 2.53×; CGFMU at 0.95×. If macro conditions deteriorate, several NCGTC schemes would require explicit subsidy.
  • 05
    Contingent liability magnitude. ₹9 lakh crore in cumulative sanctions across CGS family, with 100% sovereign cover on ECLGS portion. A second systemic shock could trigger simultaneous invocations.
Final Research Verdict

Yes — CGTMSE and ECLGS materially expanded credit access and rescued MSME credit from COVID collapse. And yes — they created measurable moral hazard concentrated in public-sector banks. But no — they have not driven the banking system toward financial unsustainability. Instead, they transferred the credit-risk perimeter from individual banks to a well-capitalised, fee-financed sovereign intermediary.

The research question assumes a trade-off between inclusion and stability. The fifteen-year data partially rejects this framing. What the schemes actually produced is a temporal redistribution of risk: the banking system's MSE NPA ratio has fallen from its FY21 peak of 22.5% to 8.2% in FY25, while CGTMSE's corpus has grown from ₹7,500 cr to ₹16,500 cr over the same window. The sovereign absorbed the shock, and the sovereign got stronger.

The real vulnerability is not today's balance sheet — it is the concentration of contingent liability in one fiscal backstop, and the behavioural persistence of the PSB-PVB moral-hazard gap. Policy priorities going forward should be: (i) risk-based differential fees to price moral hazard into the scheme itself; (ii) mandatory minimum collateral tranches for guarantee eligibility beyond a threshold; (iii) explicit counter-cyclical corpus rules that build reserves in good years; and (iv) published stress tests simulating simultaneous invocation scenarios.

India did not avoid the credit-access / credit-quality trade-off. It bought time, at a known fiscal cost, to let the growth denominator catch up with the NPA numerator. The strategy is working — but it is not costless, and it is not finished.
